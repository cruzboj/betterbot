In a small village nestled between rolling hills and dense forests, there lived a miller and his son, Roger. Roger was known throughout the village for his kindness and his dreams of adventure. He had never left the village, but tales of far-off places and the grandeur of the king’s court had sparked his curiosity. One day, the king of the land summoned Roger to the castle.

The king was a stern man, his face lined with age and his eyes sharp with wisdom. He greeted Roger with a nod and said, “Millar’s son, I have heard of your courage and your kindness. You are about to marry my daughter, the princess, but there are three tasks you must complete before I grant you my consent. Only then may you enter the princess’s chambers.”

Roger’s heart raced with both excitement and fear. “What are the tasks, my lord?” he asked, his voice steady despite his nervousness.

The king smiled, a rare sight on his usually stern face. “The first task is to fill a barrel with water from a well at the edge of the forest, but you must do it in a single day. The second task is to fetch a single grain of wheat from a field where the entire field has been planted with stones. And the third is to catch a fish from a river that is so far away, it takes a full day to reach it on foot.”

Roger stood there, his mind reeling. These were impossible tasks, as much as he knew he could accomplish them, he was well aware that they were beyond the abilities of any man. But he could not refuse the king’s challenge. With a nod, he accepted.

The next day, Roger set out for the well. He carried his bucket, his arms aching with the weight of it, yet he walked with determination. The forest was vast, and the well seemed to be at the end of a never-ending path. As the sun began to set, he arrived, and with a final dash, he filled his bucket and carried it back to the castle.

The king watched in silence as Roger arrived, the bucket clinking softly against his arm. “You have completed the first task,” the king said. “But your journey is not over.”

The second task was even more challenging. He trekked through the vast farmlands, each step seeming to lead him into another field where only stones lay. As the sun set, he felt his hope begin to fade, but he persisted, fingers digging into the soil hoping to find a grain of wheat amongst the stones. By nightfall, he found it, a tiny grain, but he carried it back to the castle, the king’s words ringing in his ears.

“Only one grain,” the king said, his eyes twinkling. “But I see your resolve.”

The morning light brought the third task. Roger set off, the distance daunting, but he walked on, hour after hour. The sun beat down upon him, and he felt his strength wane with each step. At last, he reached the river, tired and weary, but in the distance, he saw a lone fish leaping gracefully in the morning mist. With a desperate throw, he caught it and brought it back, his arms shaking with the effort.

Tired and struggling, he stood before the king. “Your majesty,” he said, his voice low, “I have done as you asked.”

The king took a deep breath and smiled. “You are a worthy son of the miller, and you possess something no wealth or force can buy—a heart of courage and a steadfast spirit. You shall marry my daughter, the princess, and together you will bring happiness to our kingdom.”

From that day on, Roger became the prince of the kingdom, and the miller’s son’s tale became a legend, passed down from generation to generation.
